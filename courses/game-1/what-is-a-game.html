What is a Game? & Light Game Theory

The Play Continuum

Before designing mechanics or writing code, we need to understand what separates a game from other forms of play and interactive media. Consider your own player history: What is the best game experience you’ve ever had? What is the most frustrating?

Interactive experiences exist on a spectrum defined by rules, goals, agency, and conflict:

Toy  →  Puzzle  →  Competition  →  Game
  • Toy: An open-ended object with no explicit goals or win conditions. Play is driven entirely by player curiosity (e.g., LEGO bricks, The Sims sandbox mode).
  • Puzzle: Has a clear goal and an optimal solution, but lacks active opposition. Once solved, it offers minimal replayability unless variables change (e.g., Sudoku, Rubik's Cube).
  • Competition: Relies heavily on physical skill, execution, and athleticism rather than purely systemic or mental choices (e.g., Basketball, Tennis).
  • Game: An interactive system bounded by rules, requiring meaningful decision-making, player agency, and conflict toward a specific outcome or goal.

Information Availability

How information is revealed or hidden dictates player decision-making, emotional tension, and cognitive strain.

Perfect Information

All players know the complete state of the game board and every possible move at all times. There are no secrets, no hidden cards, and no element of luck.

  • Examples: Chess, Checkers, Go, Connect 4.
  • Player Psychology: Fosters pure responsibility and mastery. Losses are attributable entirely to player strategy rather than chance.
  • Design Risk (Analysis Paralysis): Because choices can be calculated, players faced with high cognitive load may freeze trying to project multiple turns ahead.
  • Design Mitigation: Introduce strict time constraints (chess clocks) or spatial boundaries.

Imperfect / Hidden Information

Players possess incomplete data. Information can be hidden by the system (e.g., fog of war, shuffled decks) or by opponents (e.g., hands of cards, secret roles).

  • Examples: Poker, Battleship, Clue, Among Us.
  • Player Psychology: Shifts strategy from pure calculation to deduction, bluffing, risk assessment, and reading human behavior.
  • Design Value: Generates suspense and drama while giving weaker players a competitive chance by preventing total calculation.

💡 Class Demo & Discussion: Sticky Note Tic-Tac-Toe

Standard Tic-Tac-Toe is a perfect information game. Now imagine playing by ranking your 9 moves on private sticky notes numbered 1 to 5, then playing turn-by-turn simultaneously using Rock-Paper-Scissors to decide turn order when moves overlap. Hiding information fundamentally transforms player emotion from mechanical calculation to prediction and suspense.

Sum-of-Payoffs: Zero-Sum vs. Non-Zero-Sum

The payoff structure defines the nature of conflict, cooperation, and social alliance in a game system.

Zero-Sum Games

A strictly competitive framework where one player's gain is exactly equal to another player's loss ($+1 + (-1) = 0$). Resources in the system remain fixed.

  • Examples: Chess, Poker (for money), Fighting games, Tic-Tac-Toe.
  • Player Feeling: Direct confrontation, high tension, and cutthroat rivalry.
  • Design Note: In multi-player zero-sum environments, defeated players may feel targeted or bitter (e.g., "king-making" scenarios where a losing player decides who wins).

Non-Zero-Sum Games

The total value in the system can expand (positive-sum) or shrink (negative-sum). Players can win together, lose together, or execute mutually beneficial trades.

  • Cooperative Games: Pandemic, Flash Point, Overcooked (Players win or lose as a team).
  • Trade & Diplomacy Games: Catan, Risk (Trading resources helps both parties progress while leaving non-trading players behind).
  • Player Feeling: Camaraderie, negotiation drama, shared panic, or strategic relief. Modern board games heavily rely on non-zero-sum structures to soften direct aggression.

Dominant Strategies & Game Balance

A dominant strategy is a choice or tactic that yields the highest payoff regardless of what opponents do. If a dominant strategy exists, player choice becomes illusory and the game becomes "solved" or uninteresting.

Designers employ several core mechanics to break dominant strategies and maintain systemic balance:

Technique How It Works Example
Triangular Balance Rock-Paper-Scissors relationship where every option beats one choice but loses to another. Spears beat Cavalry, Cavalry beats Infantry, Infantry beats Spears.
Dynamic Costs Increases the resource cost of an action if it is overused or repeatedly selected. Auction mechanics or escalating mana costs for powerful abilities.
Controlled Randomness Inputs or outputs are subject to probability, forcing players to adapt rather than execute rote scripts. Drawing random card hands in Dominion or UNO.
Asymmetry Gives players distinct win conditions, abilities, or resource constraints. Root, Cosmic Encounter.

Trust & Traitor Mechanics (The Prisoner's Dilemma)

When game rules incentivize individual self-interest over mutual cooperation, social tension naturally emerges. Consider the classic game theory paradox:

Two suspects are arrested. If both stay silent, each gets 1 year. If one betrays the other while the second stays silent, the betrayer goes free and the silent partner gets 5 years. If both betray each other, both receive 3 years.

  • The Paradox: Rational self-interest (snitching) leads to a worse collective outcome than mutual cooperation.
  • Design Application: Games like Secret Hitler, Betrayal at House on the Hill, and Dead of Winter leverage this paradox to create social deception, temporary truces, and paranoia.
  • Interactive Exploration: Explore interactive payoff structures directly via The Evolution of Trust.
